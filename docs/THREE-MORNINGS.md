## 2026-11-01 — The argument Adii outgrew

Adii Pienaar · WooThemes · 2-minute read

In the early years of WooThemes, Adii Pienaar heard a threat inside an argument about software licensing. The South African co-founder was trying to build a business selling WordPress themes. When people insisted that themes should use WordPress’s open-source licence, the GPL, he understood them to mean that he should not be allowed to make money from his work.

That is how he described it in a 2014 oral-history interview. His concern was recognisable: if customers could legally share what they bought, what protected the business? He had put time and skill into designs. He wanted that work to support something real. From that position, a discussion about users’ freedoms could feel like a discussion about taking his future away.

But those were different questions. The GPL grants freedoms to use, modify and redistribute software; it does not prohibit charging for it. Commercial theme sellers could sell a useful package of design, updates and support while honouring the licence. Brian Gardner had already made that change with Revolution. Other theme businesses watched to see what would happen.

In June 2009, WooThemes moved to the GPL. The WordPress project’s published history records the change alongside iThemes. Looking back, Pienaar called his earlier belief that Matt Mullenweg did not want him to make money “absolutely absurd.” He described a communication gap, and acknowledged that his own defensiveness and appetite for online arguments had made it worse.

The useful part of this episode is not that one side won a debate. A founder was able to revise the explanation he had been living inside. His wish to earn a living did not have to disappear. His account of what other people meant could change. By the interview, he described the GPL as a non-issue for him.

> When you have faults, do not fear to abandon them.
>
> — Confucius, *Analects*, Book I, chapter VIII, paragraph 4; James Legge translation

Confucius’s line here makes correction sound like an ordinary responsibility. That is a demanding kindness: you are allowed to have been mistaken, and you are still responsible for what you do once you see it. Abandoning a position need not mean abandoning yourself.

Caspal, work in a laboratory teaches a particular respect for corrected results. You do not improve a result by defending the first reading more passionately. Building talQen may sometimes ask for the same steadiness. A colleague’s objection, or a customer’s unexpected response, might contain useful information even when it arrives awkwardly. You can listen without making an immediate promise to change everything.

If you are tired today, you do not need to reopen every decision. One small assumption is enough. There is room to be both committed to the work and less certain about your first explanation of it.

**One small invitation:** Ask one colleague: “What might I be misunderstanding about this?” Then listen before explaining your position.

## 2026-11-02 — The two friends who kept the press

Benjamin Franklin · Franklin’s printing business · 3-minute read

Benjamin Franklin’s printing business was in danger of losing the tools that made it a business. A merchant was owed money for the printing equipment. The expected funding had not all arrived, and the merchant sued. If the debt could not be paid, the press and type could be sold to settle it.

This was the small Philadelphia shop Franklin had started with Hugh Meredith. In his autobiography, Franklin recalled that Meredith’s father had supplied a hundred pounds, but another hundred remained due. Work and prospects were not the same thing as cash available on the day a creditor needed it. That distinction was becoming painfully practical.

Two friends, William Coleman and Robert Grace, approached Franklin separately. According to his account, each offered enough money to help him take over the business. Neither knew of the other’s offer. There was a condition to the proposed rescue: they did not want him to remain in the existing partnership.

Franklin did not immediately agree. He believed he owed the Meredith family consideration for what they had already done, and would have done if they could. He wanted to leave room for the original arrangement to work. Eventually he spoke directly with his partner about their circumstances. Meredith said his father really could not provide more money, and that he wanted to return to farming.

They agreed terms in writing. Franklin would take on the company’s debts, reimburse the money Meredith’s father had advanced, and make the other agreed payments. He then accepted help from both friends, taking half of what he needed from each, paid the debts and continued in his own name. His memoir places this around 1729; the later historian Holland Thompson describes the transition at age twenty-four, pointing to about 1730. The exact year is less secure than the sequence.

> no one would choose to live without friends though he should have all the other good things in the world
>
> — Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics*, Book VIII, chapter 1, 1155a; D. P. Chase translation. Clause excerpt.

Aristotle’s discussion of friendship here puts something larger beside the financial rescue. Friends are not simply another form of working capital. Their value does not begin when a business needs saving, and helping someone does not make the helper an instrument of that person’s ambitions. The account is moving partly because Franklin remembered the kindness as kindness.

Caspal, it is easy for building to become a private accounting exercise: what you owe, what you can finish, what you must somehow find. This story has a negotiation, an agreement and borrowed money in it. It also has people who noticed a difficulty and came closer.

You may not need a financial rescue today. You might need someone to read a difficult message, think through a decision, or sit with you for a few minutes without requiring a progress report. Accepting that company does not make your commitment to talQen smaller. There is a person doing this work, and that person deserves friendships with room for more than the work.

**One small invitation:** Ask a trusted friend for ten minutes of company or perspective. You do not need to turn the conversation into a progress update.

## 2026-11-03 — The design the client did not want

Brian Gardner · Revolution / StudioPress · 2-minute read

A real-estate client rejected Brian Gardner’s design. By then Gardner was making websites around a day job, and he thought this particular design was good. It looked less like a simple blog and more like a complete website. He had done the work. The person it was meant for did not want it.

Gardner’s route into software had been gradual. He worked as a project manager at an architectural firm that designed laboratory spaces for schools and hospitals. He learned WordPress by opening theme files, organising the code so he could understand it, and changing things. In his oral history, he remembered regularly breaking the site as he learned.

Free themes brought requests for paid customisations. Those requests grew into larger jobs outside working hours. The rejected real-estate design belonged to that uncertain period: a useful skill was beginning to earn money, but there was not yet a settled product business.

He did not immediately discard the design or declare that the client had failed to understand him. He put the idea on his blog and asked whether anyone would be interested. According to his account, hundreds of comments arrived. That response gave him a different piece of evidence from the rejection. One client’s requirements and a wider group’s needs were not necessarily the same.

Gardner packaged the design for distribution and put it on sale at RevolutionTheme.com. The WordPress project’s history dates Revolution’s launch to August 2007. In the interview he remembered selling $10,000 in the first month. That is a retrospective founder’s account, rather than an audited sales figure, but the larger change is clear: work intended for one customer became a product offered to many.

> Men are disturbed not by things, but by the views which they take of things.
>
> — Epictetus, *The Enchiridion*, Section V; Thomas Wentworth Higginson translation, revised Liberal Arts Press edition

Epictetus’s observation here helps separate an event from the verdict we attach to it. “This client declined this design” is information. “This work has no value” is a much larger conclusion. The philosophy has a limit here: a rejection can contain a real warning about quality or fit. It deserves investigation, not a cheerful reinterpretation on command.

Caspal, I like how modest Gardner’s next step was. He did not need a complete new identity that afternoon. He asked whether the work might help someone else, and let other people answer. The answer could have been no. Asking was still a sensible way to learn.

With talQen, a feature that does not fit one laboratory may need revision, a smaller use case, or a place on the shelf. You do not have to turn every disappointment into a breakthrough. You can simply avoid giving it more authority than the evidence supports. Some work will be useful later; some can be put down without making the effort you gave it foolish.

**One small invitation:** Choose one recent “no.” Write the specific fact it tells you, without adding a verdict about your ability.
