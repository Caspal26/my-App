Data protection in Kenya: five steps every business should take

Data Protection3 minute read

The Data Protection Act, 2019 applies to almost every organisation that collects or uses personal data in Kenya, from banks and hospitals to schools, retailers and small online businesses. Compliance is not only about avoiding penalties. It is also about earning the trust of customers, employees and partners.

The Act is enforced by the Office of the Data Protection Commissioner (ODPC), which can investigate complaints, issue enforcement notices and impose administrative fines. Below are five practical steps that put an organisation on a sound footing.

1. Find out whether you must register

The Act requires data controllers and data processors to register with the Data Commissioner, and the Data Protection (Registration of Data Controllers and Data Processors) Regulations, 2021 set out who must do so. The thresholds turn on factors such as annual turnover and number of employees, and organisations in certain sectors must register regardless of their size. Registration is renewable, so diarise the expiry date.

2. Map the personal data you hold

You cannot protect what you have not identified. Record what personal data you collect, where it comes from, why you need it, where it is stored, who can access it, who you share it with and how long you keep it. Pay particular attention to sensitive personal data, such as health information, which attracts additional safeguards.

3. Make sure each use has a lawful basis and a clear notice

Personal data must be processed lawfully, fairly and transparently, for explicit and specified purposes, and limited to what is necessary. Identify the lawful basis for each activity, whether consent, performance of a contract, a legal obligation or legitimate interests, and tell people about it in a clear privacy notice at the point you collect their data. Where you rely on consent, be able to show that it was given and make it easy to withdraw.

4. Secure the data and manage your suppliers

Put appropriate technical and organisational measures in place: access controls, staff training, encryption where suitable, and secure disposal. Where a supplier processes personal data on your behalf, such as a payroll, cloud or IT provider, have a written contract that sets out its data protection obligations. Where processing is likely to result in a high risk to individuals, the Act requires a data protection impact assessment before you begin.

5. Be ready for requests and breaches

Individuals have rights, including to be informed, to access their personal data, to object to processing and to have inaccurate data corrected or deleted. Have a simple process for recognising and responding to these requests.

If a personal data breach creates a real risk of harm, the Act requires the data controller to notify the Data Commissioner within 72 hours of becoming aware of it, and in appropriate cases to tell the affected individuals. A written breach response plan, with named people responsible, makes that deadline achievable.

What happens if you get it wrong

The Data Commissioner may impose administrative fines of up to KES 5 million or 1% of the preceding year’s annual turnover, whichever is lower, and individuals may seek compensation. Reputational damage can be more costly still.

How we can help

We help organisations assess whether registration is required, prepare privacy notices and policies, review supplier contracts, carry out impact assessments and respond to breaches and ODPC enquiries.
