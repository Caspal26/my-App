Buying property in Kenya: a due diligence checklist

Real Estate2 minute read

Land is often the largest investment a family or business makes, and land fraud remains a real risk. Most disputes can be traced to checks that were skipped before money changed hands. This checklist sets out what a careful buyer verifies before signing and paying.

1. Conduct an official search

Obtain an official search from the relevant land registry to confirm the registered owner, the size and description of the land, and any registered encumbrances such as charges, cautions, restrictions or inhibitions. Many registries now provide searches through the Ardhisasa online platform. Compare the result carefully with the copy of the title the seller has shown you.

2. Verify the seller and their authority

Confirm the identity of the seller against the register. If the seller is a company, check its registration details, its directors and the board resolution authorising the sale. If the owner has died, the property can only be sold by the legal representatives of the estate once the grant has been confirmed.

3. Check consents

  • Spousal consent: where the property is matrimonial property, the consent of the seller’s spouse may be required.
  • Land Control Board consent: transactions in agricultural land in land control areas generally require the consent of the Land Control Board, applied for within the statutory time limits.
  • Lessor’s consent: for leasehold property, the lease may require the lessor’s consent to a transfer.
  • Chargee’s consent: if the property is charged to a bank, arrange how the charge will be discharged at completion.

4. Confirm rates and rent are paid

Ask for a rates clearance certificate from the county government and, for leasehold land, a rent clearance certificate from the lessor. Outstanding rates and rent can become the buyer’s problem.

5. Inspect the property and its surroundings

Visit the land, confirm the beacons with a licensed surveyor, and check who is in occupation. Review zoning and approved plans with the county planning department, and look out for road reserves, riparian land and wayleaves that may restrict use.

6. Agree a clear sale agreement

A written agreement should set out the price, the deposit and how it is held, the completion period, the completion documents the seller must deliver, and what happens if either party defaults. Paying the balance through an advocate’s undertaking or another secure arrangement protects both sides.

7. Budget for transfer costs

The buyer usually pays stamp duty, currently 4% of the value of property within a municipality and 2% elsewhere, assessed after a government valuation, together with registration fees and legal fees. Confirm the applicable rates at the time of your transaction.
